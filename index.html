Gradient descent, visually

To find the bottom of a valley in the fog, feel which way is uphill and step the other way.

The gradient: a derivative in each direction

Setup: f takes a vector w = w0w1 and returns a number. The partial derivative ∂f/∂wk is how f changes when only wk moves: use the 1-D rules, holding the other input constant.

∇f(w) = ∂f / ∂w0∂f / ∂w1
  1. ‖∇f‖ measures how quickly f changes (long arrow: steep).
  2. ∇f points in the direction of greatest increase.
  3. ∇f = 0 at critical points.
  4. ∇(f + g) = ∇f + ∇g (every loss is a sum over samples).

a: constant vector, A: constant square matrix, g: scalar input, scalar output. Click a row to explore that rule.

Fitting a line by walking downhill

Goal: choose the intercept w0 and slope w1 of the line ŷ = w0 + w1x with the smallest mean squared error (MSE).

Approach: start anywhere. The gradient ∇MSE points the way MSE rises fastest, so take a small step against it, and repeat.

MSE(w) = 1n ∑i (yi − w0 − w1xi)²
∂MSE∂w0 = −2n ∑i ri ∂MSE∂w1 = −2n ∑i rixi w ← w − η ∇MSE(w)

The error ri = yi − ŷi is the red segment from sample i to the line. Every w = w0w1 is one line (right) and one point on the error surface (left); η is the step size.

How big a step?

Goal: find the w that minimizes f(w).

Approach: the gradient points uphill, so a step against it goes down. The step size η sets how far: too small and it crawls, too large and it overshoots the bottom, or even climbs out.

w ← w − η ∇f(w) parabola: f(w) = a(w − w*)²

The 2D bowl is steepest along one axis (curvature 1) and shallowest along the other (curvature 1/κ). The condition number κ says how stretched the bowl is: at κ = 1 it is round.

Try this

  • Exercise 1 is the in-class activity. Compute ∇f by hand at w = [0, 0]ᵀ, [2, 0]ᵀ and [3, 1]ᵀ, then use Jump to (or drag the point) to check. Where is the arrow longest? Where does it vanish? Why?
    AnswerLongest far from [2, 1]ᵀ, where f is steepest; it vanishes at [2, 1]ᵀ, the minimum, where ∇f = 0.
  • How does each arrow meet the contour line through its point?
    AnswerAt a right angle. Along a contour f does not change, so the direction of greatest increase has no part along it.
  • On the linear rule, why are all the arrows the same?
    Answer∇aᵀw = a does not depend on w.
  • On the quadratic rule, set A01 = 1, A10 = 0, then A01 = 0.5, A10 = 0.5. Does the picture change? Why?
    AnswerNo: wᵀAw only sees A01 + A10, which is why the gradient uses A + Aᵀ.
  • Still on quadratic, make both diagonal entries negative, then just one. How do the contour labels show a maximum, and a saddle?
  • On the chain rule, why are the contours straight lines, and every arrow parallel to a?
    Answerf depends on w only through aᵀw, so it is constant along lines where aᵀw is; the factor g′ only stretches the arrow a.
  • On the sum rule, watch the blue and purple arrows add tip to tail. Raise λ: where does the minimum go?
  • Click far from the bottom and press Gradient step a few times. Which way does the orange step arrow point, compared with the contour lines?
    AnswerPerpendicular to the contour line through w: straight downhill from where you stand. That is usually not straight at the minimum.
  • Drag the point w along the bold contour line. MSE stays the same, but the line on the right changes. Find two very different lines with the same MSE.
  • Hold Shift and drag w left and right: only the intercept changes, and the line slides up and down. Now up and down: only the slope changes, and the line pivots about x = 0. With the Offset at 5, why does a small slope change cost so much more MSE than at offset 0?
    AnswerThe line pivots about x = 0, far from the data, so a small turn swings it away from every sample at once. That is the steep direction of the long thin valley.
  • Drag w in a small circle around the bottom. Which way does MSE rise fastest? Check against the dashed gradient arrow.
    AnswerAlong the gradient (dashed arrow), at right angles to the contour line.
  • Set η in each zone of the colored bar under the slider and press Run. What does the walk do in each?
  • With x on Raw, slide the Offset up to 5. Your η is now in the red zone: lower it into the green and press Run. Why does the bowl become a long, thin diagonal valley?
    AnswerThe data sit far from x = 0. Raising the slope while lowering the intercept pivots the line about the middle of the data, which barely changes MSE: that pair of moves is the valley floor. Switch x to Centered: subtracting the mean of x undoes the offset, and the valley is gone.
  • At offset 0, raise κ and watch the zones bar: the largest safe η shrinks. At a safe η, how many steps does Run take? Switch x to Standardized and try again.
  • Drag the line's square handles until it matches the dashed best line. Watch your w walk to the bottom of the surface.
  • On the parabola, is f′(w) positive or negative to the right of the bottom? Which way does the step go?
  • Find the η that lands at the bottom in one step. What is it, in terms of a?
    Answerη = 1/(2a): the step −η · 2a(w − w*) is then exactly −(w − w*).
  • Pick an η that zig-zags, then one that blows up. Now raise the curvature a: the same η is no longer safe.
  • On Two valleys, start on the right. Find one η that ends in the right valley and another that ends in the left.
  • On the 2D Stretched bowl, find the largest η that converges and count the steps. Why does it zig-zag across the valley but crawl along it?
    AnswerOne η serves both directions. It must be small enough for the steep direction (or it diverges), which makes it tiny for the shallow one.
  • Rotate the bowl with θ. Does the number of steps change?
    AnswerNo. Gradient descent depends only on the bowl's shape, not which way it faces: the path just turns with it.
